I Spent Six Years Caring for My Mother’s Memory. Now I Catch Myself Checking My Own.

By Eleanor Voss Updated August 2026 12 min read

I’m not a doctor. I’m not a neuroscientist. I’m a 67-year-old retired school secretary who spent six years watching my mother disappear a little at a time — and I was terrified of becoming the story I already know by heart.

If that sentence landed somewhere heavy in your chest, I think you already know why you clicked on this.

The Woman I Used to Call When Something Went Wrong

My mother raised four kids mostly on her own. She remembered everyone’s birthday, everyone’s allergies, the name of the teacher who was unkind to me in third grade thirty years after it happened. For most of my life, she was the one who remembered things so the rest of us didn’t have to.

The first sign was small. She told me the same story about my father twice in one phone call, twenty minutes apart, word for word, like she’d never told it before. I laughed it off. Everyone repeats a story now and then.

It wasn’t one dramatic day after that. It was a hundred small things, spread across three years, each one easy to explain away on its own. The stove she’d left on twice in one month, until my brother quietly installed a shutoff timer and never mentioned why. The same question asked four times in an afternoon, each time with the same surprised interest in the answer. The morning she called me from the parking lot of a grocery store she’d shopped at for thirty years, her voice small in a way I’d never heard before, because she couldn’t remember which direction led home.

By year four, I was managing her medication schedule, her bills, her calendar. By year five, I was the one telling her stories about her own life, watching her listen to them like they belonged to someone else. I don’t say any of this for sympathy. I say it because I think you might already know exactly what I mean — from the other side of it, or maybe from both.

If you feel this quiet fear

I’m not writing this to tell you my mother’s whole story. I’m writing it because somewhere in the middle of caring for her, I started watching myself the way I used to watch her — and I don’t think I’m the only one who does that in the quiet of the morning.

The Afternoon in the Parking Garage

Two years ago, I misplaced my car in a multi-level parking garage.

Not lost — misplaced. I stood there for a good ten minutes, walking one concrete row, then another, feeling a very specific kind of cold dread move through my chest. Because I’d stood exactly like this before, except it had been my mother standing there, turning in a slow circle, and I had been the one walking the rows to find her.

I found my car. I told myself it happens to everyone in a big garage. But I got in and sat there with my hands on the wheel for five minutes before I could make myself start the engine.

That was the moment I stopped pretending it was nothing. It wasn’t about losing the car. It was that for ten minutes, I hadn’t been able to tell which version of this story I was in — the one where this happens to everyone, or the one my mother had been in, right up until the day it stopped happening occasionally and started happening always.

“Am I becoming her?” I asked my husband that night. He didn’t have an answer. Neither of us wanted the honest one.

What I Actually Found, In Plain Terms

I started reading medical papers late at night after my husband was asleep, the way you research something you’re too embarrassed to bring up with your doctor yet.

Here is the mechanism, in plain English.

Your brain runs on glucose the exact same way an engine runs on fuel. To get that fuel into your brain cells, it has to pass through microscopic doors called insulin receptors on the surface of your neurons.

Recent 2026 clinical findings revealed that over the decades, trace environmental exposure (especially heavy metals like cadmium found in tap water, air, and soil) builds up in neural tissue. It creates an oxidative “rust” directly over those receptor locks.

Think of rust inside a padlock. The key still fits. The fuel (glucose) is right there in your bloodstream. But the door will not open. And when a brain cell is starved of fuel, it shuts down room by room — names on the tip of your tongue first, then train of thought, then whole afternoons.

That was the realization that changed everything: you cannot exercise a brain you are not feeding. Crosswords were useless because they were asking a starving engine to run faster. Supplements were failing because they were pouring fuel at a locked door.
